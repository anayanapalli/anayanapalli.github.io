title: "Difficulty of Being Good"
pubDate: 2026-10-10

**Note:** This post is a review of the book <a href="https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/6971900-the-difficulty-of-being-good" target="_blank">The Difficulty of Being Good</a> by <a href="https://www.goodreads.com/author/show/170980.Gurcharan_Das" target="_blank">Gurcharan Das</a>.

Ever since I was a kid, I have been fascinated by stories.

I grew up with the Ramayana and the Mahabharata. Then came Harry Potter, Pokemon, X-Men, Justice League, Batman, Superman, Lord of the Rings, Game of Thrones, Breaking Bad, Better Call Saul, House of the Dragon, and pretty much every other popular TV series or fantasy world you can think of. If something became culturally significant, there is a good chance I watched it.

And after accumulating all of these stories over the years, I keep coming back to the Mahabharata.

Maybe I am biased, but I genuinely think it is one of the most morally complex stories ever written.

There are so many great characters on both sides. There is no clean distinction between good and evil. There are good people who do terrible things and people on the wrong side who possess extraordinary qualities. There is manipulation, loyalty, betrayal, gratitude, resentment, status anxiety, righteousness, cowardice, generosity, revenge, and sacrifice. Almost everybody is compromised in some way.

And I think that is precisely what makes the Mahabharata so fascinating.

When I was younger, I used to look at Krishna and think, "He's very manipulative" (kind of like Odysseus in The Odyssey). The Pandavas repeatedly break rules that, under ordinary circumstances, should not be broken. Krishna is clearly involved in tilting the balance in their favour. But as you get older, you start to realize that this misses the larger picture.

The war itself was unjust.

The Pandavas are playing a game in which the rules have already been corrupted. And Krishna understands that if they insist on playing according to a perfectly rigid conception of fairness, they will lose. So he manipulates the situation, but he does so with an extraordinary amount of restraint. He is not simply saying, "Do whatever it takes." There are still boundaries. There is still an attempt to preserve some larger moral order.

You see this repeatedly throughout the Kurukshetra war. When great warriors on the Kaurava side are defeated, Krishna does not simply treat them as villains who deserve whatever happens to them. Their dignity is preserved. Their greatness is recognized. Even while Krishna is trying to ensure that the Pandavas survive and ultimately prevail, there is still an underlying respect for the people standing on the other side.

That is what makes Krishna such an incredible character.

His wisdom is probably unmatched, but so is his ability to understand people. And his generosity is extraordinary. His willingness to manipulate does not come from some casual disregard for morality. It comes from an understanding of how complicated morality actually is.

Karna is probably the clearest example of this complexity.

Karna has extraordinary qualities, but he also has an enormous amount of status anxiety. He has been rejected by institutions and society his entire life because of his perceived identity and birth. And then Duryodhana becomes the person who recognizes him when almost nobody else does.

That creates an almost impossible sense of gratitude.

Duryodhana is extremely unfair to the Pandavas, but his relationship with Karna is very different. There is something genuinely pure about that relationship, and you can understand why Karna feels such profound loyalty toward him.

Even when Krishna offers Karna the opportunity to cross over, tells him about his true identity, and offers him the possibility of becoming king, Karna refuses. He chooses loyalty to Duryodhana over what would, from another perspective, be the morally correct decision.

Ethically, you can argue that Karna makes the wrong choice.

But emotionally, you understand and approve it.

And that is the Mahabharata.

It constantly puts you in situations where you understand why someone is doing something even when you cannot agree with what they are doing.

Karna's generosity is another example. The story of him giving away his armour and earrings at a crucial moment in the war is not simply a story about a vain powerful warrior. It tells you something fundamental about the kind of person he is. He is willing to sacrifice something extraordinarily valuable to him because generosity is more important to him than self-preservation.

And that is why Karna receives such high admiration from almost everyone including his arch enemies.

Even today, many Indian parents are very comfortable naming their sons with the name 'Karan' despite the tragedy of his story.

But what I really enjoyed about The Difficulty of Being Good is that it does not allow the Mahabharata to collapse into the usual simplistic categories.

You start looking at characters you might otherwise overlook.

Vidura, for example, is willing to put his own position at risk to speak about law and righteousness to people above him who have power and have no interest in hearing him.

Bhima has his own moral code, his own sense of loyalty and justice, and his own way of responding to what happens around him.

Yudhishthira is constantly trying to do the right thing, even when his commitment to righteousness creates enormous problems for himself, his wife and his brothers.

And Arjuna is particularly interesting.

When you are young, you tend to look at Arjuna and think: this is the greatest archer in the world. He is powerful, he is skilled, he is one of the great heroes.

But the more you think about him, the more you realize that his greatest quality may not actually be his ability with a bow.

It is that he questions himself during critical moments despite having the utter competence to perform the task in front of him.

The entire Bhagavad Gita begins with Arjuna confronting an almost unbearable moral question. He is about to kill people he knows and loves. He is being asked to do something enormously consequential, and instead of simply charging forward with resentment due to the way the Kauravas treated them, he stops and asks whether what he is about to do is actually right.

That capacity for self-questioning is a form of strength.

And perhaps that is one of the central ideas running through the Mahabharata. The people who are most interesting are not necessarily the people who have the cleanest answers. They are the people who understand how difficult the questions are.

Arjuna is constantly questioning himself.

Karna is driven by identity and gratitude.

Yudhishthira is driven by his conception of righteousness.

Duryodhana is driven by pride and jealousy.

Ashwathama is driven by revenge and anger.

Krishna is trying to preserve a larger moral order in a world where the ordinary rules have already broken down.

Bhishma is driven by a strict adherence to the law and his vows no matter what.

Drona is very careful about preserving his position and keeping the existing legal and social order intact.

And once you start looking at the characters this way, you realize there are far more interesting characters in the Mahabharata than just Arjuna or Karna.

That is what this book helped me appreciate.

It gave me a richer way of looking at a story I thought I already knew.

This is a big book, but I had a lot of fun reading it. More importantly, it made me think about the Mahabharata differently. It gives you a way to examine the characters not simply as heroes and villains, but as human beings caught inside extraordinarily difficult moral circumstances.

And perhaps that is the real difficulty of being good.

It is easy to be good when the choice is obvious.

The difficult part is trying to be good when every available choice has something wrong with it.

If you are interested in the Mahabharata, Dharma, morality, or simply understanding why people do the things they do, I think this is a very worthwhile book.

One warning, though: you really should know the Mahabharata beforehand. A lot of what makes the book interesting comes from the details, the relationships, and the moral dilemmas surrounding the characters. If you already know the story, you can appreciate what the author is doing at a much deeper level.
